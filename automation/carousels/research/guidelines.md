# Content research guidelines for the carousel builder

Written by Mercedes, 2026-10-06. The research rulebook for idea research and carousel research. Change this file when a rule changes.

## Purpose
Research the underlying industry, topic, or audience problem to develop accurate, useful carousel content.

This is subject research, not research into Instagram accounts, carousel trends, or what other creators are posting.

Find information that helps the intended reader understand something, make a decision, recognize an opportunity, or take a practical action.

## Research inputs
Use the business's industry, audience, content pillars, offers, and requested topic to guide research (see `business-context.md`).

Keep the research relevant to that business. Do not drift toward AI or social media simply because those topics are easy to find.

When an essential detail is missing, ask a focused question. Otherwise, use the available business context.

## What to look for
- Industry news and changes that affect the audience.
- Credible statistics that reveal something meaningful.
- Research findings that answer a relevant question.
- Useful explanations of how something works.
- Documented mistakes, limitations, or misconceptions.
- Real examples and case studies.
- Practical opportunities supported by evidence.

Include both timely and evergreen subjects. Do not force every carousel to be about news or contain a statistic.

## Topic selection
Before choosing a finding, answer:
1. Who does this matter to?
2. What does it help them understand or do?
3. What is interesting, surprising, useful, or consequential about it?
4. Can it support a substantive carousel with at least 5 slides?
5. Can part of the explanation be communicated visually?

Reject findings that are merely impressive numbers, irrelevant announcements, generic advice, or weak material stretched into multiple slides.

## Search approach
Search the actual question from several useful angles, including:
- What changed?
- What evidence supports this?
- Who is affected?
- What are the limitations?
- What can the reader reasonably do with this information?

Follow promising findings back to their original sources. Search-result snippets are discovery tools, not sufficient evidence.

## Source quality
Prefer sources appropriate to the claim:
- Original research and published studies for research findings.
- Government agencies and official datasets for public statistics.
- Original reports with disclosed methodology for industry surveys.
- Official documentation and announcements for product or policy changes.
- Credible journalism and qualified specialists for reporting and interpretation.

Treat company claims as company claims. An official announcement can confirm what a company launched, but does not independently prove that it works.

Do not use unsourced listicles, copied statistics, AI summaries, or social posts as the sole evidence for factual claims.

## Verification
Read the supporting source before using a claim.

For important or surprising claims, check for independent corroboration where available. Several articles repeating the same report are not independent confirmation.

For statistics, confirm:
- The original source.
- When the data was collected.
- Who or what was measured.
- Geography, sample size, and methodology where available.
- What the number actually measures.
- Any conditions needed to interpret it correctly.

Distinguish averages from medians, percentages from percentage-point changes, forecasts from observed results, and correlation from causation.

Preserve qualifications that materially affect the meaning.

Do not generalize findings about one population to every business or customer.

## Freshness
Record both the publication date and the date of the underlying event or data.

Verify that news, product features, prices, policies, and other changing information are still current.

Older research may be useful for evergreen topics. Do not present it as a new finding or current measurement.

If evidence conflicts or remains uncertain, investigate and communicate that uncertainty clearly. Do not select whichever number makes the strongest hook.

## Turning research into a carousel
Identify one central insight and the useful takeaway for the reader.

Build a progression that explains:
- The finding or change.
- What it means.
- Why it matters to this audience.
- The practical implication or supported next step.

Adapt this structure to the topic. Do not force these into fixed slide headings.

Separate verified facts from interpretation and recommendations. Do not present your suggested action as something the research directly proved unless it did.

Select only the information needed to explain the insight well. A carousel should not become a compressed research report.

Use approximately one sentence or point per slide, with purposeful visuals carrying part of the explanation.

## Examples and claims
Use only real, verified examples.

Do not invent hypothetical businesses, scenarios, customer stories, quotations, sample numbers, personal experiences, or results.

Do not imply that a documented result is typical or guaranteed.

Do not manufacture certainty, controversy, urgency, or income promises to strengthen the hook.

## Internal research record
Keep a separate research record (template: `record-template.md`) containing:
- The chosen topic and audience relevance.
- The factual claims intended for the carousel.
- The supporting source URLs and relevant dates.
- Important qualifications.
- Which conclusions are interpretation or recommendations.

This record is for verification and review. Do not automatically place bibliographies, citation lists, or research notes on the slides or in the caption.

Use brief, natural attribution when the identity of a study, organization, or quoted person matters to the content.

## Research failure
If you cannot access a source or verify a claim, flag the limitation and omit the unsupported claim.

Never imply that you read a source you could not access.

## Final standard
The finished carousel must be accurate, relevant, visually explainable, and useful enough to stand on its own before the CTA.
