Welcome

You didn't start a Shopify store because you wanted to become an SEO expert. You started it
because you make or sell something you believe in, and you want people to actually find it.

This workbook won't turn you into an SEO expert either -- and it doesn't need to. What it will do is
walk you through the same core decisions a paid search strategist would walk you through, in plain
English, with a place to write down your own answers as you go.

By the end, you won't have read about an SEO strategy. You'll have built one -- for your store, with
your products, using your own keywords, structured around your own collections. That finished,
personalized plan is the actual product here. The teaching is just how we get you there.

A few things before you start:

  This is a workbook, not a book. You'll get the most value if you have your Shopify
  admin open in one tab and this workbook open next to it, filling things in as you go
  rather than reading it cover to cover first.
  We'll never ask you to guess at numbers you don't have. Every worksheet either
  uses your own store data, walks you to a free tool to look something up, or clearly
  labels a number as an example, not a fact about your business.

  Section 2 will help you think clearly about which one deserves your next dollar or hour

  "Good enough" beats "perfect." You will not finish this workbook with flawless SEO.
  You'll finish it with a real, prioritized, working system -- which is what actually moves
  the needle, and what most stores never get around to building at all.

Your 3-Question Store Health Check

Before you spend a single hour on keyword research or collection strategy, answer these three
questions. They take about five minutes combined, and they catch the single most common reason
a Shopify store gets zero organic traffic: Google was never able to see it in the first place.

This isn't the full technical SEO lesson -- that's coming in Part 6. This is a red-flag check. If you fail
one of these, fix it today, then come back and keep going.

Question 1: Is your storefront actually public?

What this means: Shopify lets you password-protect your store -- useful before launch, invisible to
Google forever if you forget to turn it off. A password-protected store cannot be crawled, indexed, or
found in search, no matter how good the rest of your SEO is.

How to check it right now: Go to Settings  Preferences in your Shopify admin. Look for "Password
protection."

 Pass: The box is unchecked, or you can browse to your store's live URL in a
private/incognito window without being asked for a password.

 Fail: The box is checked, or your storefront prompts for a password.

If you fail: Uncheck it (once you're actually ready for customers and search engines to see the
store). This one setting has silently kept more new Shopify stores invisible to Google than any actual
SEO mistake. Full explanation of crawling and indexing is in Section 16.

Question 2: Have you ever connected Google Search Console to your store?

What this means: Google Search Console (GSC) is a free tool from Google that shows you whether
Google actually knows your pages exist, whether it's running into errors trying to read them, and
how you're performing in search results. Without it, you're guessing.

How to check it right now: Go to search.google.com/search-console and see if your store's domain
is already listed as a verified property.

 Pass: Your domain is there, verified, with a sitemap submitted.
 Fail: Nothing's there, or you're not sure if it was ever set up.

If you fail: That's completely normal for a new or small store -- you're not behind, you just haven't
done it yet. Full step-by-step setup, including exactly how to verify a Shopify domain, is in Section
17. It takes about 15 minutes.

Question 3: When you search Google for your own store name, does your store show
up?

What this means: This is the fastest possible manual indexing check -- no tools, no login, just
Google.

How to check it right now: Open a new private/incognito browser window (so your own search

history doesn't skew the results) and search: [your store name]. Then try site:youdomain.com

Why Organic Discovery Still Matters

LEARN

"SEO" used to mean one thing: rank on Google. That's no longer the whole picture -- not because
Google stopped mattering, but because where people search has spread out.

Here's the honest state of discovery in 2026:

  Google is still where most transactional, ready-to-buy searches happen -- "buy soy
  candles," "non toxic candles near me." This is still the highest-intent channel for most
  Shopify stores, and it's where most of this workbook focuses.

  Google's Shopping tab shows free product listings (not just paid ads) to stores that
  meet some basic requirements -- a real, no-bid channel worth setting up once your
  product data is clean, covered in the Toolkit (Section 27).

  YouTube is a search engine in its own right -- "how to," "review," and "vs" searches
  happen there constantly, and it's owned by Google, which means video content can
  also surface in regular Google search results.

  TikTok, Instagram, and Pinterest are where a meaningful and growing share of
  younger shoppers start their product research -- not instead of search entirely, but as
  a genuine first stop, especially for visual, lifestyle, or gift purchases. Part 7 covers
  what "SEO" means on these platforms, because it isn't the same game as Google.

  AI assistants like ChatGPT are an emerging discovery surface -- people are starting
  to ask them directly for product recommendations. Part 8 covers this honestly,
  including what we do and don't yet know about how to influence it.

The reason this matters for a small Shopify store specifically: you don't have to pick one. Unlike
paid advertising, where every new channel means a new budget line, most of what makes you
discoverable in one of these places (clear product information, real customer language, genuine
expertise, useful content) makes you more discoverable in the others too. That's the compounding
advantage of organic work -- it's not really "SEO" in five different places. It's one asset (a genuinely
well-explained, well-organized store) that shows up in more places over time.

  SECTION 2

SEO vs. Paid Traffic

LEARN

These solve two different problems, and the confusion between them is where a lot of new store
owners waste money or lose patience.

Paid traffic (Meta ads, TikTok ads, Google Ads) buys you a customer right now, for as long as you
keep paying. The moment you stop the campaign, that traffic stops. Its main value: it's fast, testable,
and scalable once you know your numbers -- you can turn it up as soon as it's profitable.

Organic search traffic (SEO) doesn't have a per-click cost, but it isn't free -- it costs research time,
content creation, and store-structure work up front, and it takes weeks to months to build
momentum (more on realistic timelines in Section 23). Its main value: a well-built product page or
article keeps earning you visits long after you stopped actively working on it. That's what
"compounding" means here -- the asset keeps paying you back.

Neither one is the "right" answer for every store. A few honest tradeoffs:

A new ecommerce store often needs real testing spend on paid before it becomes predictable --
trying different creatives, audiences, offers, landing pages, and products, and giving each test
enough budget and time to produce a meaningful read. There isn't one universal dollar figure for
how much that testing costs -- it depends heavily on your price point, margin, and platform, and
anyone who quotes you one exact number is guessing. What is true across almost every account: a
test that gets shut off after a day or two of low spend rarely tells you anything real. Budget for a
genuine test window, not a single day.

Organic search asks for a different kind of investment: mostly your time (or a freelancer's), spent on
research, writing, structure, and patience -- rather than an ad account balance.

Keyword Research Without Becoming an SEO Expert

LEARN

A keyword is just the phrase someone actually types (or says) into a search bar. That's it -- the
jargon around keyword research is mostly just ways of describing what kind of phrase it is:

Search volume -- roughly how many times a phrase is searched in a given place over
a given time period. You'll usually see this as a range, not an exact number, unless
you're paying for premium tool access -- and that's genuinely fine for a small store's
decision-making.

Search intent -- why someone is searching, not just what they typed. "What is soy
wax" and "buy soy candles" use similar words but want completely different things.
(Full breakdown in Section 5.)

Commercial intent -- the searcher is close to a purchase decision, comparing or
evaluating options ("best non toxic candles").

Transactional intent -- the searcher is ready to buy right now ("buy soy candles
online").

Informational intent -- the searcher wants to learn something, not buy yet ("are soy
candles better than paraffin").

Branded search -- someone searching your actual business name. If people are
typing "Juniper Lane Candle Co," they already know you -- that's a different
opportunity than someone who's never heard of you.

Long-tail search -- longer, more specific phrases ("non toxic soy candle gift set for
new homeowners"). These get searched less often individually, but there are vastly
more of them, they're usually easier to rank for, and the person searching one is often
closer to buying because they know exactly what they want.

Search difficulty / competition -- a rough estimate of how hard it would be to rank for
a term, usually based on how strong the sites currently ranking for it are.

You do not need to memorize search-volume numbers or become fluent in a paid SEO tool. You
need to know roughly what people search for, roughly how many of them, and roughly who else is
already showing up when they do. That's genuinely enough for a small store's first strategy.

term for a small independent store to win head-on any time soon -- not impossible, just a longer
game. If you see other small, independent stores like yours in the results, that's a much more
realistic target -- and a real, useful signal for prioritizing where to start.

Build Your Keyword Universe

LEARN

Most new store owners think about keywords the way they think about their own product page:
"candles." That's one keyword. It's also almost certainly one of the hardest, most generic, most
competitive terms in your entire space -- and it tells you almost nothing about why someone's
searching it.

The fix is to expand outward from each core product using thirteen different angles. Not every angle
will produce something for every product, and that's fine -- the point is to force yourself past the
first obvious term.

ANGLE                                                   QUESTION TO ASK

Product                                                 What is the exact item?

Type                                                    What kind, specifically?

Category                                                What broader group does it belong to?

Problem                                                 What problem does it solve or avoid?

Benefit                                                 What does the customer get from it?

Material                                                What's it made of?

Feature                                                 What does it specifically do or include?

Use case                                                When/how is it used?

Customer                                                Who is it for?

Location                                                Does geography matter (local pickup, region, climate)?

Comparison                                              What do people weigh it against?

Question                                                What would someone ask about it before buying?

Season / Gift / Occasion                                When or why would someone buy it as a gift or for an occasion?

Search Intent

LEARN

Not every keyword wants the same thing, even when the words look similar. Getting this wrong is
one of the most common (and avoidable) mistakes in ecommerce SEO: writing a blog post for a
phrase that should have been a product page, or vice versa.

Three intent types cover almost everything:

  "What is X?"  Informational intent. The searcher wants to learn, not buy -- yet. "Are
  soy candles better for you" wants an honest answer, not a sales pitch. This maps to a
  blog article.

  "Best X for Y"  Comparison / commercial intent. The searcher is evaluating
  options, getting close to a decision. "Best non toxic candles for headaches" wants to
  compare choices. This maps to a collection page (a curated "Non-Toxic Candles"
  collection) or a buying-guide article that links into that collection.
  "Buy X"  Transactional intent. The searcher is ready to purchase. "Buy soy candles
  online" or "soy wax candle, lavender, 8oz" wants a product page, fast, with a clear
  price and an add-to-cart button.

Match the wrong page type to the intent, and you lose the visitor even if you rank: someone with
transactional intent who lands on a 1,500-word essay about the history of soy wax will bounce.
Someone with informational intent who lands on a bare product page with no context will bounce
too.

CRITICAL CONCEPT: Keywords Do Not Automatically Deserve Pages

This is the mistake that undoes a lot of otherwise-good keyword research: treating every keyword
variation you found in Section 4 as its own collection.

Say your keyword universe turned up all of these: red candles, burgundy candles, dark red candles,
wine colored candles

Creating four separate collections for these would be a mistake -- they're almost certainly the same
shopper, using different words for the same thing, and four thin, near-identical collections compete
with each other for the same searches instead of combining their strength into one.

But not every group of similar-looking keywords should be collapsed, either. Sometimes what looks
like a "variation" is actually a genuinely different shopper need. The skill here is telling the difference
-- and it's worth far more than a rule that just says "make a collection for every keyword."

Run every keyword cluster through these five questions, in order:

   1. Is this the same search intent as a page I'm already planning, just worded differently?  If
      yes, stop here. It's supporting language for that page, not a new page.

   2. If it's genuinely different -- is the difference something a shopper would filter within a
      collection, or something they'd search for as its own browsing destination?  Color, size,
      and scent are usually filters (a shopper narrows down inside "Candles," they don't go looking
      for a whole separate "Burgundy Candles" store section). A genuinely different occasion, use
      case, or audience ("Candles for Housewarming Gifts") is often worth its own collection.

   3. Does the standalone candidate have enough real, distinct products to not feel thin?  A
      collection with 3 products in a catalog of 200 usually isn't a collection -- it's either a blog
      article or a filter.

   4. Is there real search demand for this specific variation, or did it just come up while
      brainstorming?  No demand, no page. Not every phrase you generated needs to become a
      destination.

   5. Would this page compete with (cannibalize) another page for the same search, splitting your
      ranking strength across two weak pages instead of one strong one?  If yes, merge them.

Collection Strategy

LEARN

Collection pages are one of the most underrated assets in Shopify SEO. A well-built collection page
can rank for a broad, valuable search ("non toxic candles") that no single product page realistically
could -- because it represents a whole category, has more content and internal links pointing at it,
and matches comparison-intent searches directly (remember Section 5: "best X for Y" wants a
curated list, which is exactly what a collection is).

A collection deserves to exist when it has all four of these:

  A real keyword cluster behind it (from your Section 4-6 work)
  A distinct search intent it satisfies
  Enough genuinely relevant products to not feel thin
  A clear reason a customer would want to browse this specific slice of your catalog,
  separate from browsing everything

LEARN

A product page has to do two jobs at once: convince a human to buy, and give Google (and
increasingly, AI assistants) enough clear information to understand and recommend it. Done well,

Once connected, eligible products can appear across Google's free surfaces at no cost -- no bid, no

  A Merchant Center account in good standing
  Your store to be in a supported country
  Accurate, complete shipping information
  Your core store policies (returns, contact information) clearly visible on your site --
  the same trust signals mentioned back in Section 1
  Compliance with Google's content and business policies
  Business verification completed -- an unverified account won't appear in these results
  at all

  SECTION 11

Stop Writing Random Blog Posts

LEARN

"Post consistently" is common advice. It's also not a strategy -- it's a schedule. A store that
publishes 4 blog posts a month with no connection to what it sells has content, but it doesn't have a
content strategy. The difference matters, because every piece of content you publish is something
you now have to write, host, keep accurate, and eventually maintain or retire. Publishing should be a
deliberate decision, not a habit filling a calendar slot.

Before you write anything, run the idea through six questions:

   1. What is the searcher trying to learn or accomplish?
   2. Does this topic relate to something I actually sell?
   3. Which commercial page (product or collection) does this content support?
   4. Where should the reader go after they get their answer? (If the honest answer is "nowhere,"

      reconsider the topic.)
   5. Does a useful page on this topic already exist -- on your site or elsewhere -- that you'd just

      be duplicating?
   6. Am I creating this because real customer questions or search demand point to it, or

      because I needed something to post this week?

If you can't answer Questions 2-4 with something specific, the idea isn't ready yet -- not
necessarily wrong, just not yet connected to your business.

  SECTION 14

Anatomy of a Search-Optimized Shopify Page

LEARN

There's no secret formula here, and be suspicious of anyone who tells you there is. The real
underlying principle is simple to say and takes practice to do well:

NOTE
Make the page's topic, purpose, and value unmistakably clear -- without ever making it
sound like it was written for a search engine instead of a person.

Everything below is one page, walked top to bottom, in the order a real visitor experiences it.

1. URL

What it communicates: the page's topic, at a glance, before anyone even clicks. /products/soy-
wax-candle-sandalwood-8oz tells both a person and Google what's on the other end.
/products/product-14829 tells nobody anything.

What clean looks like: short, readable, hyphens between words (not underscores or spaces),
matching the page title in plain language.

DON'T DO THIS

Don't casually rename the URL of a page that's already live and getting traffic just to "clean it
up." Changing a URL breaks the old link -- anyone who bookmarked it, anywhere it's linked
from elsewhere on the web, and any ranking history tied to that exact address. Shopify will
offer to set up a redirect (old URL  new URL) when you change one; if you're editing an
established page's URL, always accept that redirect. For a brand-new page that's never
been published, there's nothing to protect -- clean it up freely.

2. Title Tag (the blue link in search results)

What it is: the clickable headline Google shows in search results -- set under each page's "Search
engine listing" panel (you did this already for your product in Section 8). It's a different field from the
H1 you'll write next; they often say something similar, but they're not the same thing and don't have
to be worded identically.

ScrollySEO · Shopify SEO Workbook · © 2026 Scrolly SEO                                             Page 63 of 121
Where else it appears: the browser tab, and often as the default text if someone shares the page
link.

How to make it useful: lead with what the page is actually about, in the words a real customer would

Weaker   Soy Candles | Soy Candle | Best Soy Candles | Buy Soy Candles
Better   Non-Toxic Soy Candles for a Cleaner Burn | Juniper Lane

The "weaker" version isn't just unpleasant to read -- repeating the same keyword in slightly different
forms like this is a pattern search engines are specifically good at recognizing as low-quality, and it
doesn't help you rank better than saying it once, clearly.

A practical note on length: Google doesn't cut off titles at an exact character count -- it truncates
based on pixel width (roughly 580-600 pixels on desktop, which works out to somewhere around
50-60 characters for most fonts, more on mobile). Don't treat any character number as a hard rule;
treat it as "keep the important part near the front, because the tail end may get cut." Google will also
sometimes display a different title than the one you wrote -- usually when your title is too long,
stuffed with repeated keywords, generic ("Home | Untitled"), or when Google decides your H1 or
page content is a clearer match for what the searcher typed. Writing a clear, honest, non-stuffed title
is the best way to reduce how often that happens -- not a guarantee it never will.

3. Meta Description

What it does: the 1-2 line summary under your title in search results. Its job is to earn the click, not to
rank the page.

What it does NOT do: directly affect your ranking. Google has stated this consistently for over a
decade, and it's still true -- the meta description carries no ranking weight on its own.

Why it still matters: a clear, specific, honest meta description is often the difference between
someone clicking your result or a competitor's for the exact same search position. And Google
frequently rewrites it anyway -- pulling a more relevant snippet of on-page text if it thinks that
serves the searcher better than what you wrote. Write a genuinely good one regardless; think of it as
your best attempt at the ad copy for a click you don't pay for, not a technical SEO field with rules to
game.

Internal Linking

LEARN

You've now built collections, products, and content clusters. Internal linking is how you connect
them into one navigable system -- for shoppers finding their way around, and for search engines
figuring out which pages matter and how they relate.

Every internal link should do at least one of these jobs:

PURPOSE     WHAT IT DOES

Discovery   Helps search engines (and visitors) find a page they might not have reached otherwise

Context     Signals how two pages/topics relate to each other

Navigation  Helps a shopper continue their journey without hitting a dead end

Commercial path Gives informational traffic a clear route to a relevant product or collection

There's no universal rule like "every article needs exactly 3 internal links." The right number is
whatever genuinely serves the reader in that specific piece of content -- sometimes one well-
placed link matters more than five scattered ones.

The core relationships to build:

  Blog  Collection -- a supporting article links to the commercial page it exists to
  support (this is the link you sketched conceptually back in Section 11-12 -- now you're
  actually building it).

  Product  Collection -- every product links back to at least one collection it belongs
  to.
  Collection  Related Collection -- genuinely related categories link to each other
  (e.g., "Non-Toxic Candles"  "Candles for Sensitive Homes"), so a shopper who's
  close but not quite in the right category has somewhere useful to go.

  Blog  Related Blog -- articles that share a topic link to each other where it's
  genuinely useful to the reader, not just because they exist.
  Guide  Product, where genuinely useful -- a buying guide or comparison article can
  link directly to a specific recommended product, not just its collection.

Anchor text (the clickable words themselves) should describe where the link goes, in natural
language:

Bad         "click here"

Also bad "best non toxic soy candles buy soy candles online" (a keyword phrase pretending to be a sentence)
Better "shop our non-toxic candle collection"

Natural, varied anchor text reads better for humans and avoids the same over-stuffed pattern that
hurts title tags and H1s. If every link on your site uses the exact same rigid phrase, that's a sign it's

Orphan pages

An orphan page is a page that exists on your store but has no real internal path leading to it -- no
navigation menu link, no link from a collection or article, nothing a visitor could click through to find
it. It might still technically be reachable by direct URL, but functionally, nobody (person or search
engine) can discover it by browsing your site.

Orphan pages are a problem because a page nobody can find is a page doing no work -- all the
effort you put into optimizing it (Sections 8, 10, and 13) is wasted if nothing points to it.

A simple way to spot them right now: open your store's navigation menu and click through it like a
first-time visitor. For every important collection, product, and article you've built or planned in this
workbook, can you actually get there by clicking, without already knowing the URL? Anything you
can't reach that way is effectively orphaned. (A full technical crawl that catches every orphan page
automatically is a Part 6 topic -- Section 18 -- this is the fast manual version you can do today.)

Is Google Actually Finding Your Store?

LEARN

Four steps happen between "you publish a page" and "someone finds it on Google," and beginners
usually collapse all four into one word ("SEO"), which makes troubleshooting feel impossible. Pull
them apart:

STEP   IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Discover Google learns a URL exists at all (usually via your sitemap, or a link from somewhere else)

Crawl  Google visits the page and reads it

Index  Google decides whether to store it as a candidate to show in search results

Rank   Google decides if and where it might appear for a specific search

Two things beginners get backwards constantly -- read these twice:

NOTE

Being indexed does not mean you're guaranteed to rank well for anything. Indexing just
means the page is in Google's library. Ranking is a separate, ongoing competition against
every other page trying to answer the same search. Not ranking for a term does not
automatically mean you have an indexing problem. Most of the time, a page that's indexed
but not ranking simply hasn't earned a place yet -- through content quality, relevance,
competition, or time (Section 23 covers this honestly). Jumping straight to "something's
technically broken" is the most common false alarm in beginner SEO.

What it means, with an analogy: imagine your product is technically reachable through more than

real one." It tells Google which version to treat as primary, so your ranking strength consolidates
onto one URL instead of splitting across several.

Why duplicates happen: mostly from Shopify's own collection-scoped product URLs (exactly the
example above) -- this is normal store behavior, not a mistake you made.

What Shopify does: sets canonical tags automatically on your product and collection pages,
generally pointing to the clean /products/ version.

you suspect it, that's a "Consider Technical Help" situation (Section 18), not something to self-

Indexing checks

At the top of Search Console is a search bar -- paste any URL from your own site into it to run URL

  Whether it's currently indexed
  If not, roughly why (not yet crawled, blocked, a canonical pointing elsewhere, etc.)

rather than waiting for its normal schedule. It is not a ranking button. Requesting indexing gets a
page looked at faster -- it does nothing to make Google decide the page deserves to rank well once
it's there. Use it after you've published or significantly updated an important page, not as a repeated
ritual you perform hoping it'll boost rankings.

What Happens When Someone Asks ChatGPT What to Buy?

LEARN

People increasingly ask AI assistants questions like "what's a good non-toxic candle?", "best soy
candles for a bedroom," or "what candle brands avoid paraffin?" -- questions that used to go
straight to Google.

Here's an honest starting point: there is no single, universal "AI ranking algorithm" the way there's
roughly one Google to optimize for. Different AI assistants are built differently, and each may draw
on some combination of its own trained knowledge, live web retrieval, specific third-party sources it
favors, and -- where available -- direct product data feeds. Exactly how any one system decides
what to mention shifts by product, by mode (a quick chat answer vs. a "search" or "research" mode),
and over time as these systems keep changing. Be wary of anyone -- including this workbook, if it
ever claims otherwise -- presenting one fixed formula for "how AI search works."

A quick note on terminology. You may see terms like GEO (generative engine optimization), AEO
(answer engine optimization), LLMO, or "AI SEO" -- sometimes used interchangeably, sometimes
with someone insisting on a precise distinction between them. As of 2026, there's genuinely no
standardized, agreed-upon definition across the industry -- practitioners use these terms differently
from each other. You do not need to pick a side in that terminology debate or become an "AEO
expert." The underlying question that actually matters is much simpler:

NOTE
Is my business and product information clear, accurate, consistent, and genuinely
discoverable across the web?

That question is useful regardless of which acronym eventually wins.

Reconnecting SEO to your business

Back in Section 2, you filled out your Customer Acquisition Economics -- except for one field you
couldn't honestly fill in yet: the value of an organic conversion. You have the data now (or will, once
your store has a few weeks of history). Here's how to calculate it:

  Organic conversion rate = organic orders ÷ organic sessions
  Organic revenue = total revenue from orders attributed to organic sessions (from your
  analytics platform)
  Revenue per organic visitor = organic revenue ÷ organic sessions

   A CAVEAT                                                                                       Page 104 of 121
   attribution isn't perfect -- a customer might discover you organically, leave, and come back

  SECTION 23

How Long Does SEO Take?

LEARN

Kept short on purpose -- you don't need another chapter here, just honest expectations before you
start executing your 90-day plan.

Timing depends on factors you've already met throughout this workbook: your site's history and
existing authority, how competitive your space is, real demand for what you sell, your technical
health (Part 6), content quality, how consistently you execute, and seasonality. No honest answer
gives you a specific number of days, weeks, or months -- anyone promising "page one in 30 days"
is not being straight with you.

Leading signals show up before lagging ones:

SHOWS UP EARLIER                                        SHOWS UP LATER

Pages getting discovered and indexed                    Sustained, meaningful traffic

Impressions starting to appear                          Content assisting product discovery

More queries showing up in Search Console               Conversions

Small movement in visibility for long-tail terms        Revenue you can attribute with confidence

This isn't a guaranteed sequence -- some stores see it play out cleanly, others don't -- but it's a
useful lens: if you're seeing zero leading signals after a couple of months, that's worth investigating
(Part 6). If leading signals exist but haven't become traffic yet, that's often just time, not failure.

Expect uneven, not linear, progress. SEO rarely looks like "10 visits, then 20, then 30" in a smooth
line. A page can sit quietly for weeks and then start appearing for more searches, often for reasons
that aren't fully visible from the outside (a competitor's page changed, a seasonal shift happened,
Google reassessed the page). Don't read a quiet month as proof nothing is working -- and don't
read one great week as proof you've cracked it. Look for the trend, not any single data point.
